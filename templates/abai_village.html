Abai Village: the neighborhood war built

Most visitors know Abai Village for one thing: the hand-pulled ferry and the abai sundae stalls along the main lane. Both are real and worth doing — but the reason this neighborhood exists at all is the part most signs skip.

What happened here

In the winter of 1950, during the Korean War, Chinese forces entered the fighting and pushed United Nations troops back south — the retreat known as the 1·4 Hu-toe (January 4th Retreat). Civilians from Hamgyong province, in what is now North Korea, fled south along with the retreating soldiers, expecting the war to end quickly and the border to reopen. When the armistice was signed in 1953, the border didn't reopen. It never has.

With nowhere else to go and their hometowns now unreachable, thousands of these displaced Hamgyong families settled on a narrow sandbar in Sokcho, across a lagoon channel from the town center. That sandbar, in the Cheongho-dong district, became Abai Village. The name itself comes from a Hamgyong dialect word for an older man — a term of address that stuck to the whole community.

Why it still feels distinct

Because the community formed almost entirely from one region of the North, Hamgyong food, dialect, and customs concentrated here in a way they didn't elsewhere in South Korea. The abai sundae you eat in the village — a blood sausage stuffed into squid or intestine casing — and naengmyeon-style cold noodles both trace back to Hamgyong cooking, distinct from the versions that became standard in Seoul.

Most of the first generation who actually fled south are now gone; the community today is largely their children and grandchildren, estimated at several thousand residents. What remains visible are the food, the layout of the village, and a small museum of restored refugee-era houses at the Sokcho City Museum nearby, which shows what the original shanty dwellings looked like.

The ferry

The gaetbae is a flat wooden boat that crosses the narrow channel between Abai Village and Sokcho's town center. It has no engine — passengers pull the boat across hand-over-hand along a fixed cable. It became widely known after the 2000 K-drama Autumn in My Heart was filmed here, which is also what first brought large numbers of tourists to the village. But the ferry predates the drama by decades: for a neighborhood built on a sandbar with the town just across the water, it was simply the practical way to get around, and still is for a lot of residents today.

Visiting well

The main food lane can feel touristy at peak hours, and prices there run higher than elsewhere in Sokcho — a natural consequence of a formerly quiet neighborhood becoming a well-known stop. Walking a few lanes back from the main strip, past the murals and narrower alleys, gives a better sense of the village as a place people actually live, not just a photo backdrop.

Nearby: the Sokcho City Museum's restored refugee housing exhibit gives useful context before or after walking the village itself.
